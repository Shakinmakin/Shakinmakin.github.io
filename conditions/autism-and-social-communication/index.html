Autism & Social Communication

Social communication is about connecting with others: sharing attention, playing, taking turns, and having conversations. Here's some general information about how speech therapy can support children's social communication, including autistic children.

What is social communication?

Social communication (sometimes called pragmatics) includes the many skills we use to interact with other people, such as:

  • Sharing attention and interest with another person
  • Using and understanding gestures, facial expressions, and tone of voice
  • Taking turns in play and conversation
  • Starting, joining, and keeping a conversation going
  • Adjusting how we talk for different people and situations
  • Flexible thinking, like handling changes in plans or seeing another person's point of view

Speech therapy and autism

Autistic children communicate in many different ways. Some speak a lot, some speak a little, some echo phrases they've heard, and some communicate through gestures, pictures, or devices. Every way a child communicates is meaningful.

At Tribe, therapy for autistic children is play-based, follows the child's interests, and respects each child's individuality. Our focus is on helping children communicate what they want to say and connect with the people around them in ways that feel good to them, not on making them appear a certain way.

Social communication challenges aren't limited to autism. Some children have difficulty with these skills for other reasons, and therapy can help them too.

Things parents sometimes notice

  • Limited eye contact, pointing, or showing things to others
  • Difficulty joining play or playing back-and-forth with other children
  • Repeating words or phrases from shows or other people
  • Difficulty with conversations, such as staying on topic or taking turns
  • Strong distress with changes in routine
  • Concerns from teachers about social interaction

If you have concerns about your child's development, talk with your pediatrician. A speech-language pathologist can evaluate communication skills as part of a child's care team, but cannot formally provide an autism diagnosis.

How speech therapy can help

Depending on the child, therapy may support:

  • Early skills like shared attention, imitation, and play
  • Expanding ways to request, comment, protest, and share ideas
  • Understanding and using language in everyday routines
  • Conversation, turn-taking, and peer play
  • AAC for children who benefit from pictures, devices, or apps
  • Coaching parents, caregivers, and teachers to support communication throughout the day

In-home, school, and community sessions let children practice in the real places they play and learn. With your permission, we're happy to coordinate with your child's other providers, such as ABA, occupational therapy, or school teams.
