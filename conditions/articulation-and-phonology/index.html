Speech sounds

Articulation & Phonological Disorders in Children

General information about speech sound challenges, such as trouble with sounds like 'l', 'r', 's', or 'th', or being hard to understand, and how speech therapy can help children speak clearly and confidently.

What is a speech sound disorder?

Learning to say all the sounds of a language takes time, and young children make many predictable errors along the way. A speech sound disorder is the general term for when a child's sound errors continue past the age when most children have outgrown them, or make their speech hard to understand.

Speech-language pathologists often describe two main types:

Articulation

Difficulty producing specific sounds, for example a lisp on /s/ ("thun" for "sun") or a distorted /r/ ("wabbit" for "rabbit").

Phonology

Patterns of errors that affect groups of sounds, for example saying "tat" for "cat" and "do" for "go," or leaving off the ends of words.

When do children learn different sounds?

Children develop sounds gradually. Sounds like /p/, /b/, /m/, and /w/ usually come early, while sounds like 'l', 'r', 's', and 'th' are among the later sounds to develop, and many children are still working on them in the early elementary years. Because every child is different, an SLP considers your child's age, the types of errors, and how understandable your child's speech is overall.

Things parents sometimes notice

  • Family members or teachers have trouble understanding your child
  • Your child is often asked to repeat themselves
  • Substituting, leaving out, or distorting certain sounds
  • A lisp, or air escaping to the side of the mouth on /s/ sounds
  • Your child seems frustrated or embarrassed about how they sound
  • Your child avoids certain words or talking in front of others

How speech therapy can help

Speech therapy for articulation and phonology is tailored to each child's specific sound patterns. Depending on the child, sessions may include:

  • Learning to hear the difference between sounds (listening games)
  • Learning where to place the tongue, lips, and teeth for a sound
  • Practicing new sounds in syllables, words, sentences, and conversation
  • Games and activities that make lots of practice feel fun
  • Simple home practice ideas so skills carry over into everyday speech
