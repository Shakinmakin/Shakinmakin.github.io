Stuttering in Children & Teens

General information about stuttering and how speech therapy can support children and teens who stutter, both in how they talk and in how they feel about talking.

What is stuttering?

Stuttering is a difference in the flow (fluency) of speech. A person who stutters knows what they want to say, but the forward flow of their speech is interrupted. Stuttering often begins in early childhood, commonly between ages 2 and 5. Many young children who begin to stutter stop over time, while others continue to stutter into the school years and beyond.

What stuttering can sound like

  • Repetitions of sounds, syllables, or short words: "b-b-b-ball," "I-I-I want"
  • Prolongations, or stretching out a sound: "ssssun"
  • Blocks, when no sound comes out for a moment even though the child is trying to speak

Some children also show physical tension, like blinking or tightness in the face, or begin avoiding certain words or speaking situations. It's also common for young children to have some typical "disfluencies," like repeating whole words or phrases, as they learn to talk. An SLP can help sort out what's typical and what may benefit from support.

Things parents sometimes notice

  • Repetitions or stretched sounds that seem to be happening more often
  • Moments where your child gets "stuck" and can't get a word out
  • Visible tension or struggle while talking
  • Your child seems frustrated, embarrassed, or says talking is hard
  • Avoiding speaking, swapping words, or talking less than before
  • A family history of stuttering

How speech therapy can help

Stuttering therapy is individualized to the child's age, needs, and goals, and to what matters to the family. Depending on the child, therapy may include:

  • For young children, working closely with parents on ways to support easy, relaxed talking at home
  • Learning strategies that can make speech feel smoother and easier
  • Building confidence and reducing worry or avoidance around speaking
  • Helping children and teens understand stuttering and advocate for themselves
  • Practicing real-life situations like answering in class, ordering food, or presentations

The goal is effective, confident communication, so children and teens can say what they want to say, wherever they are.
